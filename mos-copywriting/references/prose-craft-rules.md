# Prose Craft Rules — WRITTEN COPY ONLY

Production rules for clean, human-sounding **written** copy: posts, emails, captions, About/sales pages, lessons — anything the audience READS. These complement the master frameworks (Sugarman, Kennedy, Ogilvy, Caples, Halbert) with specific anti-patterns and quality gates drawn from real writer feedback.

> **Scope split (2026-06-11):** Scripts read ALOUD (Reel/Short VO, YouTube scripts, talk tracks) use `spoken-script-rules.md` instead. The repetition and rhythm anti-patterns below are written-surface rules — spoken word WANTS anaphora and repetition (a listener can't scan back; repetition is spoken formatting). Don't lint script VO with this file.

---

## Empathy First

Sugarman says sell on emotion. Halbert says write conversationally. This rule operationalises both: before you present anything, acknowledge where the reader is right now.

- Enter the conversation already happening in their mind (Robert Collier Principle). Don't start with what YOU want to say; start with what THEY are thinking
- The first 2-3 sentences of any piece should acknowledge the reader's situation, problem, frustration, or goal
- Use "you" before "we". The reader's world comes first, the brand's solution comes second
- BAD: "Our platform offers enterprise-grade security features for modern teams"
- GOOD: "You've seen the headlines. Another breach, another company scrambling. If you're responsible for your team's data, that knot in your stomach is justified"

**When to apply:** Every copy type. Headlines, emails, landing pages, social posts, sales letters. If the opening doesn't acknowledge the reader, rewrite it.

## Conversational Tone (Beyond Halbert)

Halbert's conversational tone principle is foundational. These are the specific mechanical checks that enforce it:

### Contractions
- Use contractions naturally: it's, don't, you'll, they're, we've, can't, won't, shouldn't
- Stiff prose signals AI or corporate committee. Contractions signal a real person
- **Check:** Scan for "it is", "do not", "they are", "you will", "we have", "can not", "will not". If more than 3 uncontracted forms appear in a piece, convert them
- **Exception:** Formal brand voice documents may specify no contractions. Honour that

### The Read-Aloud Test
- Read every sentence aloud. If it sounds awkward spoken, rewrite it
- If you wouldn't say it in conversation, don't write it
- This catches: passive voice, overly complex sentences, unnatural word order, stiff transitions

### First-Person / Second-Person Balance
- "You" is the most important word in copy (Sugarman Axiom 12 reinforced)
- "We" sparingly for brand voice. Never "one" or "the reader"
- "I" for personal stories, testimonials, founder letters

## Punctuation Anti-Patterns

### Em Dashes: At Most One Per Piece (Zero Preferred)

Use at most ONE em dash (—) in a whole piece, and only where the dramatic pause genuinely earns it. Zero is the default and always safe.

The tell is not the single deliberate em dash — it's the *recurring* em-dash aside. AI-generated copy reaches for it in every other sentence (3-5x more often than skilled human writing), so a string of them is one of the strongest "a machine wrote this" signals a reader pattern-matches on. One, used once, reads as a human choice. Three read as a bot.

Hard rule: never use a hyphen as a substitute for the same aside. Fix the structure, don't downgrade the punctuation. Restructure every aside beyond the one allowed em dash into:

| Instead of em dash | Use |
|---|---|
| Parenthetical aside | Commas |
| Connecting related clauses | Semicolons |
| Creating emphasis via separation | Periods (two shorter sentences) |
| Truly parenthetical info | Parentheses |
| Introducing explanations | Colons |

- BAD: "The platform — which took three years to build — handles millions of transactions"
- GOOD: "The platform handles millions of transactions. It took three years to build"

### Ellipses: Banned in Body Copy
- Never use ellipses (...) in body copy. They create trailing, uncertain tone
- Exception: dialogue or quoted speech where trailing off is intentional
- Seeds of curiosity (Sugarman) don't need ellipses. "But here's where it gets interesting." works better than "But here's where it gets interesting..."

### Semicolons: Limited
- Maximum 1-2 per section. Overuse creates dense, academic prose
- If you've used a semicolon, ask: "Would two sentences be clearer?" Usually yes

### Exclamation Marks: Rare
- Maximum one per piece of copy (landing pages, emails, ads)
- Sales letters/VSLs: maximum 2-3 total, reserved for genuine excitement
- Never in headlines (screams desperation)

## Repetition Anti-Patterns

### Phrase Repetition
- No 3+ word phrase should appear 3+ times in any single piece (excluding the product name, brand name, and primary keyword/concept)
- **Check method:** After drafting, search for any phrase you've used as a habitual crutch. Common AI offenders: "in order to", "it's important to", "this means that", "when it comes to"
- On 2nd+ occurrences, vary the language. Same idea, different words

### Differentiator Repetition
- If a brand's key selling point has been stated once clearly, don't repeat it verbatim in subsequent sections
- One strong mention > three diluted mentions
- If you must reference it again, approach from a different angle: first mention states the feature, second shows it in action, third quotes a customer about it

### Sentence Structure Repetition
- Never start 3+ consecutive paragraphs the same way (e.g., "This means...", "[Product] offers...", "You can...")
- Vary openings: statement, question, scenario, quote, data point, "you" address
- After a pattern of medium sentences, drop a short one. After two short ones, let one breathe longer

## Rhythm and Momentum

Sugarman's slippery slide and Element 13 (rhythm) are the theory. These are the mechanical checks:

### Sentence Length Variation
- Short sentences punch. Medium sentences explain and connect. Longer sentences build momentum through parallel structure, but they must earn their length
- Never string three sentences of the same length together
- **The staccato triad is now an AI tell — use rarely, never as a default.** The "{Word}. {Word}. {Word}." fragment burst ("Faster setup. Lower costs. Better outcomes.") and the anaphoric "{adj} X, {adj} Y, no Z" ("Real systems, real results, no theory") were once punchy; overuse by AI has turned them into slop signals. At most ONCE in a whole piece, and only when all three beats carry genuinely distinct, concrete content that a flowing sentence would weaken. Never as a closer or a section sign-off. When tempted, write one straight sentence instead. A flowing imperative list joined with "and" ("Join free, learn the tool, and put your busywork on autopilot") is fine — that's a natural series, not the fragment-staccato.
- After a dense technical paragraph, follow with something lighter

### Section Hooks
- Open each section with a hook: a surprising fact, a relatable problem, or a bold claim
- Don't frontload all the interesting material and leave the end trailing off
- End paragraphs with a thought that leads naturally into the next (Sugarman's slide, mechanised)

### Pacing by Section
- Introduction: 60% empathy/problem, 40% promise
- Body: alternate between evidence-heavy paragraphs and lighter narrative/example paragraphs
- Close: tighten rhythm. Shorter sentences. Build urgency through pace, not through fake scarcity

## Story and Specificity

Sugarman Trigger 26 (storytelling) and Trigger 29 (specificity) combined into production rules:

### Concrete Examples Required
- Every major section (or every 300-400 words in long-form) should contain at least one:
  - Specific example with named details
  - Mini case study (even 1-2 sentences)
  - Illustrative scenario the reader would recognise
- "Show don't tell" through specifics: "A Melbourne warehouse cut cooling costs by 34% after switching" not "switching saves money"
- Name real scenarios the reader would recognise from their own experience

### Specificity Escalation
- Level 1 (weak): "Many companies see results"
- Level 2 (better): "Companies see a 20% increase"
- Level 3 (strong): "Buffer increased trial-to-paid conversion from 2.4% to 4.1% in 90 days"
- Always aim for Level 3. Drop to Level 2 only when Level 3 data isn't available. Never ship Level 1

## Honesty and Nuance

Kennedy's damaging admission and Sugarman's Trigger 2 (honesty) as production rules:

- Acknowledge trade-offs and limitations honestly. This builds trust and credibility
- BAD: "[Product] is perfect for every business"
- GOOD: "[Product] works best for mid-size teams with 50-200 employees. If you're a solo operator, [simpler alternative] might be a better fit"
- Readers trust content that admits where the boundaries are
- A damaging admission early builds trust for every claim that follows
- The "Not Ideal For" framing works in all copy types, not just comparison tables

## EEAT Signals for Copywriting

These credibility signals improve conversion across all copy types, not just SEO content:

### Source Your Claims
- Every significant claim should be attributable. "According to [Source]" beats unsupported assertions
- Valid sources: industry reports, peer-reviewed research, named experts, verified review platforms, government statistics
- Name sources within the text. A linked footnote is worth less than "Forrester's 2025 report found..."

### Kill Vague Marketing Language
- Never use without attribution or data: "best", "leading", "world-class", "cutting-edge", "industry-leading", "game-changing", "revolutionary"
- These are Ogilvy's "brag and boast" and Sugarman's cliche warning combined
- Replace every vague claim with a specific, verifiable detail
- Test: could someone fact-check this claim? If no, rewrite with specifics

### Author/Brand Credibility
- Copy should establish why THIS brand/person has authority on this topic
- Specific credentials beat generic titles: "12 years building enterprise security products" not "industry expert"
- Match expertise to the topic being discussed

## Filler Phrases: Kill on Sight

These phrases add zero value. Remove every occurrence:

- "it's important to note"
- "it goes without saying"
- "in today's world" / "in today's landscape"
- "at the end of the day"
- "needless to say"
- "it's worth mentioning"
- "as we all know"
- "when it comes to"
- "the fact of the matter is"
- "in order to" (just use "to")
- "due to the fact that" (just use "because")
- "at this point in time" (just use "now")
- "on a daily basis" (just use "daily")
- "a wide range of" (just use "many" or be specific)

## Australian English (When Applicable)

When writing for Australian audiences or Australian brands:

### Spelling
- -ise not -ize: optimise, organisation, analyse, recognise
- -our not -or: colour, behaviour, favour, honour
- -re not -er: centre, metre, fibre, litre
- -ence not -ense: licence (noun), defence, offence
- -lled/-lling: travelled, cancelled, modelling

### Common Differences
- programme (not program, except software)
- grey (not gray)
- enquiry (not inquiry, for general questions)

### Punctuation
- Single quotes for quotation marks as primary style
- Periods and commas outside quotation marks unless part of quoted material
- Oxford comma: use consistently for clarity

**When to apply:** Only when the client or audience is Australian. For US/UK audiences, use their respective conventions. When in doubt, ask.
