# Ogilvy Body Copy & Advertising Rules

Source: Ogilvy on Advertising (David Ogilvy, 1983)

---

## The Research-First Method

Ogilvy never started with copy. He started with homework.

**The Rolls-Royce story:** Spent 3 weeks reading about the car. Found the statement "at sixty miles an hour, the loudest noise comes from the electric clock" buried in engineering notes. That became the headline — followed by 607 words of factual copy. The ad ran in just 2 newspapers and 2 magazines ($25,000 total). Ford based their next multi-million dollar campaign trying to beat that claim.

**The Mercedes story:** Sent a team to Daimler-Benz HQ in Stuttgart. Three weeks taping interviews with engineers. Campaign of long, factual ads increased US sales from 10,000 to 40,000 cars/year.

**The principle:** The more you know about the product, the more likely you are to come up with a big idea. "If you are too lazy to do this kind of homework, you may occasionally luck into a successful campaign, but you will run the risk of skidding about on the slippery surface of irrelevant brilliance."

## The Big Idea Test

Five questions to test if your idea is big enough:

1. Did it make me gasp when I first saw it?
2. Do I wish I had thought of it myself?
3. Is it unique?
4. Does it fit the strategy to perfection?
5. Could it be used for 30 years?

No idea is big unless it will work for thirty years. The Hathaway eyepatch ran from 1951. Dove's "doesn't dry your skin" ran from 1955. Marlboro's cowboy ran 25+ years.

## Body Copy Rules

### Write to one person, not a crowd [UNIVERSAL]
"Pretend you are writing each of them a letter on behalf of your client. One human being to another, second person singular." Queen Victoria preferred Disraeli to Gladstone because Disraeli talked to her like a human being, not a public meeting.

### You cannot bore people into buying [UNIVERSAL]
You can only interest them. If the copy isn't interesting, it won't be read, and it won't sell.

### Short sentences, short paragraphs, simple words [UNIVERSAL]
Ogilvy used "obsolete" in Dove copy — most housewives didn't know the word. Changed it to "old-fashioned." Write in the language people use in everyday conversation.

### Specifics over generalities [UNIVERSAL]
"Don't write essays. Tell your reader what your product will do for him or her, and tell it with specifics." Vague claims convince nobody.

### Use story form [UNIVERSAL]
"Write your copy in the form of a story." The Zippo ad ("The amazing story of a Zippo that worked after being taken from the belly of a fish") and Caples' "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" are landmarks because they use narrative.

### Avoid analogies [UNIVERSAL]
Gallup found analogies are widely misunderstood. "Just as plants require moisture, so too does your skin" — readers don't complete the equation. They get confused about what you're selling.

### No brag and boast [UNIVERSAL]
"Stay away from superlatives like 'Our product is the best in the world.'" Gallup calls this Brag and Boast. It convinces nobody.

### Use testimonials [UNIVERSAL]
"Readers find the endorsements of fellow consumers more persuasive than the puffery of anonymous copywriters." Expert testimonials beat celebrity ones. Amateur delivery beats polished performance — more credible.

### Include the price [PRINT-SPECIFIC]
When price is left out, people turn the page. Digital equivalent: always show pricing or at least a price anchor on landing pages and sales pages.

### The "positively good" approach [UNIVERSAL]
For parity products, don't claim superiority. Just say what's good about your product — clearly, honestly, informatively. "Sales will swing to the marketer who does the best job of creating confidence that his product is positively good."

### Repeat your winners [UNIVERSAL]
"If you are lucky enough to write a good advertisement, repeat it until it stops selling." Readership doesn't decline through at least four repetitions. You're advertising to a moving parade, not a standing army.

## Long Copy vs. Short Copy

Ogilvy's position: long copy sells more than short for most products.

**Evidence:**
- Merrill Lynch: 6,450-word ad pulled 10,000 responses without a coupon
- Schlitz beer: 5 pages of text moved brand from 5th to 1st in sales
- Shell gasoline: 800-word newspaper ads reversed a 7-year market share decline
- Mercedes: long factual ads quadrupled US sales

**The rule from direct response:** In split-run tests, long copy invariably outsells short copy. Direct response advertisers — who know to a dollar what each ad sells — always use long copy.

**The caveat:** Long copy must be well-written. Your first paragraph must be a grabber. Max 11 words for the opening paragraph. [UNIVERSAL]

**Digital equivalent of long copy:** Detailed landing pages, long-form sales letters, comprehensive product pages. The principle holds — more facts, more sales. [UNIVERSAL]

## Visual Selection Rules

### Story appeal in photographs [PRINT-SPECIFIC]
Photos that arouse curiosity ("What goes on here?") dramatically outperform static images. Digital equivalent: thumbnails and hero images that create an open loop.

### Before-and-after [UNIVERSAL]
Gallup found not a single before-and-after campaign that failed to increase sales across 70 campaigns studied.

### Photographs beat drawings [PRINT-SPECIFIC]
More readers, more believable, better remembered, more coupons pulled. Digital equivalent: real screenshots and photos outperform illustrations for credibility.

### Make the product the hero [UNIVERSAL]
"There are no dull products, only dull writers." Show the product itself with utter simplicity when possible.

### End-result imagery [UNIVERSAL]
Show what happens AFTER using the product. Finished dish beats raw ingredients. Clean house beats dirty one.

### Simple focus [UNIVERSAL]
One person, not crowd scenes. Don't enlarge faces bigger than life size. Keep it focused.

## Layout & Typography Rules That Affect Copy Performance

### Reading order: illustration > headline > copy [PRINT-SPECIFIC]
Put them in that order top-to-bottom. Headlines below illustrations are read by 10% more people. Digital equivalent: hero image first, then headline, then copy — matches natural scroll behavior.

### Editorial layout over ad layout [UNIVERSAL]
Ads that look like editorial content get roughly 6x the readership of ads that look like ads. Use serif type, three columns, captions under images, drop initials.

### Captions are read 4x more than body copy [UNIVERSAL]
Never use an image without a caption. Include brand name and promise in every caption.

### Typographic boosters for long copy [PRINT-SPECIFIC]
- Subhead between headline and body (appetite builder)
- Drop initial increases readership 13%
- First paragraph max 11 words
- Cross-heads every 2-3 inches — some interrogative
- Bold or italic for key paragraphs
- Bullets, arrows, numbered lists for unrelated facts
- Line spacing between paragraphs increases readership 12%

Digital equivalents: all of these apply directly to web copy, email, and landing pages.

## What Makes Great Advertising (Ogilvy's Philosophy)

1. **Advertising is not entertainment or art.** "When I write an advertisement, I don't want you to tell me that you find it 'creative.' I want you to find it so interesting that you buy the product."

2. **Sell, or else.** "If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative." (Benton & Bowles, endorsed by Ogilvy)

3. **Make the product the hero.** Not the copywriter's cleverness.

4. **The consumer is not a moron.** "She is your wife." Respect the reader's intelligence. Inform them. Don't insult them with puffery.

5. **Every ad is a long-term investment in brand image.** Every ad should contribute to the same consistent personality, year after year.

6. **Direct response is the truth serum.** DR advertisers know what works because they measure everything. Follow their lead: long copy, strong offers, clear calls to action, tested headlines.
