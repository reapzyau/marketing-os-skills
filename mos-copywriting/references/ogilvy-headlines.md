# Ogilvy Headlines Reference

Source: Ogilvy on Advertising (David Ogilvy, 1983)

---

## The 5x Rule

On average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy.
If your headline doesn't sell, you've wasted 90% of your money.

This is the single most important principle in advertising copywriting. The headline IS the ad for 80% of your audience.

APPLY WHEN: Writing any headline for any medium. This is the non-negotiable starting point.

---

## Headline Types That Work

### 1. Benefit Headlines
Promise the reader something they want: whiter wash, more miles per gallon, freedom from pimples, fewer cavities.

**Template:** "[Product] gives you [specific benefit]"
**Template:** "How [product] [delivers specific result]"
**Template:** "Get [desirable outcome] with [product]"

APPLY WHEN: You have a clear, provable product benefit. Default to this type when in doubt.

### 2. News Headlines
Announce something new: a new product, an improvement, or a new use for an existing product. Ads with news are recalled by 22% more people than ads without.

**Template:** "Announcing: [new thing]"
**Template:** "Introducing [product] — [what's new about it]"
**Template:** "Now you can [do something previously impossible]"
**Template:** "Finally, [solution to known frustration]"

APPLY WHEN: You have genuine news — a launch, update, new feature, new use case, or new result.

### 3. How-To Headlines
Headlines offering helpful information attract above-average readership. People want to learn.

**Template:** "How to [achieve desired outcome]"
**Template:** "How [specific person/role] [achieved specific result]"
**Template:** "How I [did something relatable] in [timeframe]"

APPLY WHEN: You're teaching, sharing a process, or the product solves a problem people actively search for.

### 4. Question Headlines
Arouse curiosity. Make the reader need to know the answer.

**Template:** "Do you [make this common mistake]?"
**Template:** "What [happens/would you do] when [scenario]?"
**Template:** "Which of these [category] mistakes do you make?"

APPLY WHEN: You want to engage readers who may not know they have a problem, or to segment an audience.

### 5. Command Headlines
Direct. Tell the reader what to do.

**Template:** "Stop [pain point]. Start [desired state]."
**Template:** "[Do this] before [consequence]"
**Template:** "Try [product] — [single compelling reason]"

APPLY WHEN: You have authority with the audience and a clear, simple action you want them to take.

### 6. Testimonial / Quote Headlines
Putting your headline in quotes increases recall by an average of 28%.

**Template:** "[Exact quote from real user about specific result]"
**Template:** "[Name/Role]: '[their words about the product]'"

APPLY WHEN: You have a strong, specific testimonial. Expert testimonials outperform celebrity ones. Real users outperform paid endorsers.

## Power Words

Ogilvy identified words that consistently trigger reader attention and response:

- **Free** — the most powerful word in advertising
- **New** — signals news, triggers curiosity
- **How to** — promises useful information
- **Suddenly** — implies a breakthrough
- **Now** — urgency without being pushy
- **Announcing** — news flag
- **Introducing** — news flag for launches
- **Amazing** — use sparingly but it works
- **Improvement** — signals upgrade
- **Wanted** — curiosity trigger
- **Bargain** — value signal
- **Hurry** — scarcity/urgency

APPLY WHEN: You need to increase stopping power. Insert these naturally — never force them.

## Specifics Over Generalities

Specifics are more credible and more memorable than generalities.

- BAD: "Sears' profit is less than you might suppose"
- GOOD: "Sears makes a profit of 5 per cent"

- BAD: "Our car is very quiet"
- GOOD: "At sixty miles an hour, the loudest noise comes from the electric clock" (Rolls-Royce)

The Rolls-Royce headline is factual, specific, and came directly from product research — not from brainstorming.

APPLY WHEN: Always. Replace every vague claim with a specific number, fact, or detail. If you don't have specifics, you haven't done enough research.

## The Quote Headline Rule

When you put your headline in quotes, you increase recall by an average of 28%.

Quotes signal a real person said this. They add credibility and story appeal. Even when the quote is from the brand, the format itself boosts performance.

APPLY WHEN: You have a strong statement that sounds natural as dialogue, or when using testimonials.

## Audience Flagging

If your product is bought by a specific group, put a word in your headline that flags them down.

Examples: "asthma," "bedwetters," "women over thirty-five," "gym owners," "freelancers."

In local newspapers, include the city name — people are most interested in what happens where they live.

Also: always include the brand name in the headline. If you don't, 80% of readers (who skip body copy) will never know what product you're advertising.

APPLY WHEN: Your product serves a defined niche. The more specific the flag, the stronger the pull on the right reader.

## Research-First Method

Ogilvy's process before writing a single word:

1. **Study the product deeply.** Spent 3 weeks reading about Rolls-Royce. Sent a team to Stuttgart for 3 weeks to interview Mercedes engineers. Read for 10 days about margarine.
2. **Study competitor advertising.** What are they doing? What's working? This gives you bearings.
3. **Research among consumers.** How do they think about your product category? What language do they use? What attributes matter? What promise would most likely make them buy?
4. **The headline comes FROM the research.** The Rolls-Royce headline ("at sixty miles an hour...") was found in engineering documentation, not invented in a brainstorm.

The big idea comes from a well-informed unconscious. Stuff your conscious mind with information, then unhook your rational thought process.

APPLY WHEN: Before every headline writing session. If you haven't done the homework, you're guessing.

## Headline Anti-Patterns

- **Tricky headlines** (puns, double meanings, obscurities) — counter-productive. Your headline competes with 350 others. Telegraph what you want to say.
- **Blind headlines** that don't say what the product is or does — 20% below average in recall.
- **No headline at all** ("headless wonders") — the silliest mistake of all.
- **ALL CAPS** — retard reading. Use lower case. Capitals have no ascenders or descenders to help word recognition.
- **Headlines superimposed on illustrations** — harder to read.
- **Period at end of headline** — full stops are called that because they stop the reader dead.

APPLY WHEN: Reviewing any headline draft. Check against this list before publishing.

---

## Long vs. Short Headlines

Starch reports headlines over 10 words get less readership. But retail ad studies found headlines of 10+ words sell more merchandise.

Ogilvy's conclusion: if you need a long headline, write one. If you want a short one, that's fine too. What matters is the content, not the length.

APPLY WHEN: Don't artificially truncate a headline that needs more words to deliver the promise clearly.
