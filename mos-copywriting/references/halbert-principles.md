# Gary Halbert's Copywriting Principles
## Distilled from The Boron Letters

---

### 1. A-Pile vs B-Pile

Everyone sorts mail (and their inbox) into two piles. A-pile looks personal. B-pile is obviously commercial. Everyone opens all A-pile. B-pile gets skipped or trashed.

**Get into the A-pile:** No teaser copy, no logos, no labels. Personal tone. Make them think: "Who sent me this?" If it looks like marketing, it gets B-piled.

**APPLY WHEN:** Email subject lines, social post hooks, DM openers, landing page headlines.

---

### 2. The Starving Crowd

"What's the #1 advantage? A STARVING CROWD." Not better copy, not a better product. A group already hungry for what you sell.

**Find them:** Watch what people DO buy, not what they SAY. Surveys lie; wallets don't. Repeat buyers who spend big money recently (recency > frequency > unit of sale). Ads that run over and over. Your own customer list beats any rented list.

**APPLY WHEN:** Choosing markets, positioning offers, picking niches. Confirm the crowd is starving before writing a single word.

---

### 3. Conversational Tone

Write like you talk. Halbert wrote his best copy as letters to his son. Simple words ("get" not "procure"), short sentences, one idea per paragraph. Transition phrases: "Now, here's the thing..." / "And, of course..." Ask questions then answer them. First person. Use "you" constantly.

**The test:** Read it aloud. Sounds like a friend talking? Keep it. Sounds like an ad? Rewrite it.

**APPLY WHEN:** Every piece of copy. The more it sounds like a real person, the more it sells.

---

### 4. Specificity

Specific details create believability. Vague claims create skepticism.

- "Friday, 6:30 p.m. June 12, 1984" vs "Recently"
- "$5,000 x 4,000 buyers = $20 million" vs "Lots of money"
- "This 361-word letter generated 7,156,000 customers" vs "Millions of customers"

**APPLY WHEN:** Headlines, proof, case studies, testimonials, social proof. The more specific the number, the more real it feels.

---

### 5. Reason Why

Always give a reason for what you're doing. Why this letter? Why this sale? Why now? People need a "because." Even a weak reason outperforms no reason.

Halbert attached a baggie of sand to his real estate letter — then explained WHY. The grabber got attention; the reason kept them reading.

**APPLY WHEN:** Discounts, launches, content shares, CTAs. Never make an offer without a reason behind it.

---

### 6. One CTA

Every piece of copy has ONE job. One action. Halbert's close was ruthlessly specific: tell them where the form is, tell them to fill it out, tell them how much to send, tell them to do it RIGHT NOW. He devoted up to 25% of entire ads to closing — leading the reader by the hand through every micro-step.

**APPLY WHEN:** Landing pages, emails, posts, sales letters. Decide the ONE action before you write. Everything exists to drive that single action.

---

### 7. Testing Philosophy

Test the big things first. These three variables dwarf everything else:

1. **List/audience** — a mediocre letter to a starving crowd beats a great letter to the wrong list.
2. **Offer** — price, guarantee, bonuses, payment terms.
3. **Headline/hook** — the first thing they see.

Don't tweak button colors when the list is wrong.

**APPLY WHEN:** Split testing, campaign planning, debugging underperformance. Check audience first, then offer, then headline.

---

### 8. The Letter Template (AIDA + Halbert)

1. **Attention grabber** — demands focus, MUST be relevant to the message.
2. **Tie-in** — connect grabber to pitch immediately. No irrelevant tricks.
3. **Interesting facts** — specifics that build curiosity and credibility.
4. **Desire** — word pictures of benefits. Make them feel the outcome.
5. **Close/Action** — step-by-step. What they gain now, what they lose by waiting.

**APPLY WHEN:** Sales pages, long-form emails, VSL scripts, webinar pitches.

---

### 9. Movement/Action in Copy

Never let the reader stop. Bucket brigades ("But here's where it gets interesting..."), open loops, one-word transitions ("Onward."), wide margins, short paragraphs, white space. Your ad should look like an exciting editorial piece, not an advertisement — editorial gets 5x the readership.

**APPLY WHEN:** Any copy longer than a paragraph. If a reader finds a natural stopping point, you've lost them.

---

### 10. Research Method

1. **Assemble everything** — competitor ads, DM pieces, books, product details.
2. **"Nugget notes"** — short fragments, single words. Don't edit. Just capture.
3. **Star the best** — one star good, two great, three red hot.
4. **Walk away** — a day or two. Breakthrough arrives during the gap.
5. **Write to AIDA formula** with your best idea.
6. **Handcopy great ads** — write proven winners by hand. Imprints flow at a cellular level.

**APPLY WHEN:** Starting any new campaign or entering a new market.

---

### 11. "Does This Make Me Want to Read the Next Sentence?"

The ultimate editing test. Go sentence by sentence. If it doesn't pull the reader forward, cut it.

Halbert's copy was engineered for one outcome: keep the reader moving toward the order. Beautiful prose that doesn't sell is worthless.

**Corollary:** "If you are writing for applause, you will go home with empty pockets."
**APPLY WHEN:** Final editing pass. Read aloud, sentence by sentence. Every sentence that doesn't pull forward gets cut.
