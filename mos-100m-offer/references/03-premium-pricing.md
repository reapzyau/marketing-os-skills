# Chapter 3 — Premium Pricing

## Hormozi's why
Price isn't a description of value — it *creates* perceived value. The wine study: identical wines rated higher when labeled more expensive. People want to buy expensive things; they just need a reason. Raising prices triggers a virtuous cycle; lowering them triggers a death spiral.

## Framework — Commit to the price BEFORE building the value stack
This is Hormozi's reversal of conventional pricing wisdom. Most people build the offer, then ask "what should I charge?" Hormozi has you commit to a premium price first, then build the value to justify it. This forces creativity in Chapters 4–10.

### The virtuous cycle (when you raise prices)
- Emotional investment ↑ — they paid more, they care more
- Perceived value ↑ — "can't be cheap if it's that expensive"
- Client results ↑ — invested people do the work
- Best clients attracted — easiest to satisfy, cheapest to serve
- Margins multiply — re-invest in systems, talent, experience
- Bank account climbs — even while reinvesting

### The death spiral (when you lower prices)
- Emotional investment ↓
- Perceived value ↓ ("must be bad if it's that cheap")
- Client results ↓
- Worst clients attracted (never satisfied)
- Margins destroyed
- Burnout → exit

### Pricing anchor heuristic
- Identify the highest-priced visible competitor (call this $X)
- A premium price is **2–10× $X**, not 1.2–1.5×
- If under 2×, the prospect mentally rounds you to "expensive but comparable" — that's still commodity territory
- If 3–10×, the prospect thinks "this must be something completely different"

The point is not the literal multiplier. The point is to create enough gap that direct comparison fails.

## Diagnostic questions (ask the user)
Use `AskUserQuestion` with options here — pricing is a discrete commitment:

**Question 1:** "What's the highest-priced visible competitor's offer, and what do they charge?"
Open-ended.

**Question 2 (AskUserQuestion):** "Where do you want to anchor your premium price relative to that competitor?"
Options:
- 2–3× competitor — entry premium
- 3–5× competitor — clear category-of-one signal
- 5–10× competitor — flagship / aspirational tier
- I want to think about this — defer (don't proceed without commitment)

**Question 3:** "What would feel uncomfortable to charge? Now: what would you charge if you knew the offer would deliver the dream outcome?"
The gap between those two numbers is usually the right anchor.

## Validation criteria (what "good" looks like)
- A specific $ price committed (not "around" or "starting at")
- Price is ≥2× the highest visible competitor
- The user can articulate WHY this price justifies itself in one sentence (foreshadow of the value stack)
- The user acknowledges the virtuous-cycle effect (they're not flinching at the number)

Push back if:
- Price is 1.1–1.9× competitor → not premium, just expensive
- User defaults to "what the market will bear" → that's commodity logic; remind them the goal is to leave the comparison set
- User wants to "test" lower prices first → reframe: the test isn't the price, it's whether the value stack justifies it

## Pitfalls (Hormozi's warnings)
- **Being the second-cheapest.** No strategic benefit. Being the most expensive has one.
- **"Testing" lower prices.** A lower price test ALWAYS converts better short-term. The trap is mistaking that for a long-term signal. Premium pricing tests the *value stack*, not the price.
- **Pricing slightly above the market.** "Slightly more expensive" creates buyer remorse without status reward. Go far enough that the buyer feels they're entering a different category.
- **The wine study generalises.** Across nearly every category, price up = perceived quality up. Don't fight the psychology.
