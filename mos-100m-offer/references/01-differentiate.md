# Chapter 1 — Differentiate

## Hormozi's why
A commodity is anything the prospect can directly compare to a competitor. Comparison forces a price-based decision, which means margin compression until the business dies. A Grand Slam Offer is "sold in a vacuum" — the prospect's choice becomes "you vs nothing," and price becomes whatever you set.

## Framework
Diagnose the current offer's commoditisation level, then map what makes it incomparable. This chapter is a diagnostic, not a build — the actual decommoditising work happens chapters 4–11.

### Commodity-status indicators (ask these about the existing offer)
1. Do prospects ask for discounts? → price-led decision
2. Do you match competitor pricing? → ceded pricing power
3. Can the offer be copy-pasted onto a competitor's site without change? → identical to market
4. Does "why you vs them?" stump the salesperson? → no real differentiation

If 2+ are true → it's a commodity.

### The lead-gen agency case study (use as concrete reference)
- **Before (commodity):** $1,000 down + $1,000/mo retainer. 0.5–1× return. Easily duplicated.
- **After (Grand Slam):** Pay once, no retainer. Cover ad spend, agency runs and works leads. Pay only when prospects show up. Guarantee 20 in 30 days or next month free. Full playbook + daily coaching + scripts + call recordings included.
- **Multiplier:** Same ad spend → 2.5× responses × 2.5× close rate × 4× price = **22.4× cash upfront.**

Pattern to extract: every "after" component either (a) reverses risk, (b) bundles deliverables, or (c) shifts payment terms. Hormozi calls this "selling in a category of one."

## Diagnostic questions (ask the user)
Ask these in conversation — open-ended, not multiple-choice:
1. "Describe your current offer in 1–2 sentences as a prospect would hear it."
2. "Who are the 2–3 competitors a prospect would compare you against, and how do you differ — in their words?"
3. "On a scale of 1–5, how often do prospects ask for discounts or push back on price? (5 = always)"
4. "If I could remove ONE objection or comparison from the buying conversation, which would have the biggest impact?"

Capture these verbatim — they become the inputs that Chapters 4 and 9 directly work against.

## Validation criteria (what "good" looks like)
A passing commoditisation diagnosis includes:
- An honest commodity score (1–5 where 5 = full commodity)
- At least 2 named competitors with the prospect's likely comparison logic
- One clearly stated "primary axis of comparison" (price, speed, expertise, location, etc.)
- A first-pass hypothesis on what would make the offer incomparable (this is a hypothesis, not the answer — the answer comes via Ch 4–11)

Push back if:
- The user says "we're not a commodity" without evidence — probe with "what do prospects compare you against, then?"
- The differentiation listed is generic ("better service", "higher quality") — those are commodity claims, not differentiators
- No competitors are named — the prospect always has alternatives, even if they're "do nothing"

## Pitfalls (Hormozi's warnings)
- **"We're already differentiated."** If prospects ask about price, the differentiation doesn't matter — they can still compare. Differentiation isn't what *you* see, it's what *they* see.
- **Differentiating on the wrong axis.** Cheaper, faster, friendlier are commodity claims (every competitor says them). Real differentiation usually involves a structural change to the offer itself — payment terms, guarantee, what's included, who delivers.
- **"There's no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest in the marketplace. But there is for being the most expensive."** — Dan Kennedy via Hormozi.
