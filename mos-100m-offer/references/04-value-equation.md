# Chapter 4 — The Value Equation

## Hormozi's why
Value has a formula. Four drivers. Two on top (increase them), two on the bottom (decrease them). To charge a premium, you must provide much more — and "more" means a higher value-equation score, not more deliverables.

## Framework — THE formula
```
              Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
Value =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
              Perceived Time Delay  ×  Perceived Effort & Sacrifice
```

Numerator: **increase** both. Denominator: **decrease** both.
Cheat code: anything divided by zero → infinity. Drive a denominator toward zero, value goes toward infinite.

### The 4 drivers — what to ask about each
1. **Dream Outcome ↑** — What will I make? What will happen? The thing they want most (beautiful, respected, powerful, loved, higher-status).
2. **Perceived Likelihood ↑** — How will I know it's going to happen? Communicated via: social proof, guarantees, third-party authority, track-record numbers.
3. **Perceived Time Delay ↓** — How long will it take? Two layers: long-term outcome AND short-term experience. Engineer both. Break long journeys with early wins.
4. **Perceived Effort & Sacrifice ↓** — What's expected of me? Effort = what they start doing. Sacrifice = what they give up. Both kill purchasing intent equally.

### Scoring rubric (each driver 0–1, total /4)
| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Driver is broken / actively reducing value |
| 0.25 | Weak — most prospects fail here |
| 0.5 | Acceptable — most competitors are here |
| 0.75 | Strong — clear competitive advantage |
| 1 | Near-perfect — the driver is a moat |

The Xanax/meditation worked example:
| Driver | Meditation | Xanax |
|---|---|---|
| Dream | 1/1 | 1/1 |
| Likelihood | 0/1 | 1/1 |
| Time delay | 0.5/1 | 1/1 |
| Effort | 0/1 | 1/1 |
| **Total** | **1.5/4** | **4/4** |

Same dream. One is a billion-dollar industry; the other isn't. The difference is the OTHER three drivers — the ones most offers ignore.

### Logical vs psychological solutions (the Ch 4 deep insight)
If a logical solution existed for the prospect's problem, someone would already have built it. What's left is psychology. Examples Hormozi uses:
- London trains: don't make them faster — add a "next train in X minutes" map
- Slow elevators: don't speed them up — add floor-to-ceiling mirrors (people look at themselves and forget)
- Pricing: don't make it cheaper — make it scarcer and pricier

What the prospect *thinks* the value drivers are matters more than what they actually are. **Perception is the product.**

## Diagnostic questions (ask the user)
1. "Score your current offer on each of the 4 drivers, 0 to 1. Be brutal."
2. "Which driver is the weakest? Why?"
3. "If you could only fix ONE driver to double conversion, which one and how?"
4. "What's a psychological solution (not logical) that could move a driver?"

## Validation criteria (what "good" looks like)
- All 4 drivers explicitly scored 0–1 with evidence (not "I think it's 0.8 because we're good")
- Total score reported with the weakest driver flagged
- One concrete plan to move the weakest driver this iteration
- One psychological (not logical) lever surfaced for at least one driver

Push back if:
- Total score ≥3.5/4 — almost no offer scores that high; the user is grading on a curve
- All 4 driven by "dream outcome" only — that's the trap (everyone has the same dream)
- Improvements proposed are all logical ("we'll add more features") — push for psychological levers (perception, certainty signals, novel proof formats)

## Pitfalls (Hormozi's warnings)
- **Only ever working on the numerator.** Most offers are built around dream outcome and (sometimes) credibility. The denominators — time and effort — are where most value gains live.
- **Confusing "more features" with higher value.** More features usually INCREASE effort/sacrifice on the prospect's side. Subtract, don't add.
- **Optimising the wrong driver.** The weakest driver caps the whole product. Don't double-down on already-strong ones.
- **"Get the bottom to zero."** This is the most important sentence in the chapter. An instant, effortless result is infinitely valuable. The closer you get, the more you can charge.

## Workbook output expected
