# Chapter 5 — Divergent Thinking (The Brick Exercise)

## Hormozi's why
Convergent thinking (one right answer) loses to divergent thinking (many right answers, one much-more-right). School trains the first; life pays for the second. Chapter 6 requires divergent thinking — this chapter is the warmup.

## Framework

### Convergent vs divergent
- **Convergent:** fixed variables, unchanging conditions, one answer (math problems). Binary right/wrong. School teaches this because it's easy to grade.
- **Divergent:** many variables (some unknown), dynamic conditions, many right answers + one *much* more right. The mode Chapter 6 requires.

### The brick exercise (literal)
120 seconds. Write down every use for a brick. Most people get the obvious: doorstop, weapon, paperweight, building block. The breakthrough answers require changing a variable (size, material, shape):
- Plastic brick → Lego, floatation device
- Drilled brick → pen holder, fish home
- Gold brick → payment, value store
- Jumbo brick → seat
- Tiny brick → jewelry

Lesson: **change a variable, the use cases explode.** Most offer breakthroughs come from changing a variable on an existing component, not from inventing new components.

### Apply it to the business
List every "brick" — every asset the business has. Then ask: what could each become if I changed its size, material, format, audience, timing, or pricing?

Examples of business "bricks":
- Time (yours and the team's)
- Expertise (specific knowledge domains)
- Audience / list / community
- Content already created
- Vendor and partner relationships
- IP / frameworks / methods
- Physical assets / equipment / locations
- Certifications and credentials
- Data and case studies
- Recordings, calls, workshops past

## Diagnostic questions (ask the user)
This chapter is brainstorm-heavy. Use conversation, not `AskUserQuestion`.

1. "List every 'brick' your business has — assets, IP, relationships, content, time, audiences. Aim for 15+."
2. "Now: which of these have you only used in one way? Pick 3 and brainstorm 3 alternative uses for each."
3. "What single asset, if combined with another in a new way, would create something the market hasn't seen?"

## Validation criteria (what "good" looks like)
- ≥15 bricks listed (small bricks count — a Slack channel is a brick)
- ≥3 bricks have ≥3 alternative uses each
- ≥1 combination idea that the user genuinely hasn't tried before
- The user can name which Ch 6 step each promising brick will likely feed into (delivery format? bonus? solution?)

Push back if:
- Brick list is <10 — they're undervaluing what they already have
- All "alternative uses" are minor variations on the current use — push for variable changes (size, material, format, audience)
- Combinations are all "add X to Y" — push for "what if X *replaced* Y?"

## Pitfalls (Hormozi's warnings)
- **Treating brainstorming as a separate skill.** It's not — it's a *mode*. Divergent thinking can be triggered with a warmup (the brick exercise) and switched off after Ch 6.
- **Filtering during brainstorm.** Don't. Filter in trim & stack (Ch 6 step 5). At this stage, quantity > quality.
- **Skipping this chapter.** Ch 6 will be flat without it. The exercise feels silly; do it anyway.

## Workbook output expected
A 15+ item brick inventory, 3 underused bricks with 3 alternative uses each, and at least one combination idea worth testing in Ch 6.
